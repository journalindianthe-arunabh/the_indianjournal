The idea of reservation in India first evolved in 1882 when Jyotirao Phule and William Hunter suggested the caste based reservation in the Education commission. Later reservation was extended to various caste and class. In 1932 Poona Pact was signed between Mahatma Gandhi and Dr. B.R. Ambedkar abandoning separate electorates for Dalits in Favor of reserved seats within a joint Hindu electorate. The issue of reservation was taken by the constituent assembly and in 1950, in which temporary reservation was provided for Scheduled Castes (SCs) and Scheduled Tribes (STs) in legislatures and public employment. The temporary reservation was for the period of ten years initially, which kept on increasing each time by constitutional amendment. Reservation in India was never just a poverty-relief programme. Its primary purpose was to address the historic inequality in opportunity, suppression and discrimination by the upper class.

In the landmark Indra Sawhney case, the Supreme Court accepted caste as one determinant of backwardness while also emphasising the need to exclude the socially and educationally advanced sections through the principle of the creamy layer. The Court also developed the well-known 50 per cent ceiling principle in the context of reservations under Article 16(4), while recognising that exceptional circumstances could require consideration.

The debate over reservation can never end in a country like India which has different caste and class biases. There are genuine concerns about its implementation, unequal distribution of benefits, the creamy layer and whether the most disadvantaged sections are receiving adequate opportunities. These concerns require periodic review, transparency and reform. Youth have every right to question the system, but the debate should be based on evidence rather than slogans. The real question should be: Has India eliminated the social inequalities that made reservation necessary? Until the answer is yes, reservation remains an important instrument of social justice.
